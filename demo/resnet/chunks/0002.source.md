<!-- block:B000025 -->
On the ImageNet classification dataset [[[IMM_I000125]]], we obtain excellent results by extremely deep residual nets. Our 152layer residual net is the deepest network ever presented on ImageNet, while still having lower complexity than VGG nets [[[IMM_I000126]]]. Our ensemble has **[[IMM_I000127]]** top-[[IMM_I000128]] error on the ImageNet _test_ set, and _won the 1st place in the ILSVRC [[IMM_I000129]] classification competition_. The extremely deep representations also have excellent generalization performance on other recognition tasks, and lead us to further _win the 1st places on: ImageNet detection, ImageNet localization, COCO detection, and COCO segmentation_ in ILSVRC & COCO [[IMM_I000130]] competitions. This strong evidence shows that the residual learning principle is generic, and we expect that it is applicable in other vision and non-vision problems.

## **[[IMM_I000131]]. Related Work**

<!-- block:B000027 -->
**Residual Representations.** In image recognition, VLAD [[[IMM_I000132]]] is a representation that encodes by the residual vectors with respect to a dictionary, and Fisher Vector [[[IMM_I000133]]] can be formulated as a probabilistic version [[[IMM_I000134]]] of VLAD. Both of them are powerful shallow representations for image retrieval and classification [[[IMM_I000135]] [[IMM_I000136]]]. For vector quantization, encoding residual vectors [[[IMM_I000137]]] is shown to be more effective than encoding original vectors.

<!-- block:B000028 -->
In low-level vision and computer graphics, for solving Partial Differential Equations (PDEs), the widely used Multigrid method [[[IMM_I000138]]] reformulates the system as subproblems at multiple scales, where each subproblem is responsible for the residual solution between a coarser and a finer scale. An alternative to Multigrid is hierarchical basis preconditioning [[[IMM_I000139]] [[IMM_I000140]]], which relies on variables that represent residual vectors between two scales. It has been shown [[[IMM_I000141]] [[IMM_I000142]] [[IMM_I000143]]] that these solvers converge much faster than standard solvers that are unaware of the residual nature of the solutions. These methods suggest that a good reformulation or preconditioning can simplify the optimization.

<!-- block:B000029 -->
**Shortcut Connections.** Practices and theories that lead to shortcut connections [[[IMM_I000144]] [[IMM_I000145]] [[IMM_I000146]]] have been studied for a long time. An early practice of training multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) is to add a linear layer connected from the network input to the output [[[IMM_I000147]] [[IMM_I000148]]]. In [[[IMM_I000149]] [[IMM_I000150]]], a few intermediate layers are directly connected to auxiliary classifiers for addressing vanishing/exploding gradients. The papers of [[[IMM_I000151]] [[IMM_I000152]] [[IMM_I000153]] [[IMM_I000154]]] propose methods for centering layer responses, gradients, and propagated errors, implemented by shortcut connections. In [[[IMM_I000155]]], an "inception" layer is composed of a shortcut branch and a few deeper branches.

<!-- block:B000030 -->
Concurrent with our work, "highway networks" [[[IMM_I000156]] [[IMM_I000157]]] present shortcut connections with gating functions [[[IMM_I000158]]]. These gates are data-dependent and have parameters, in contrast to our identity shortcuts that are parameter-free. When a gated shortcut is "closed" (approaching zero), the layers in highway networks represent _non-residual_ functions. On the contrary, our formulation always learns residual functions; our identity shortcuts are never closed, and all information is always passed through, with additional residual functions to be learned. In addition, highway networks have not demonstrated accuracy gains with extremely increased depth (_e.g._, over [[IMM_I000159]] layers).

## **[[IMM_I000160]]. Deep Residual Learning**

<!-- block:B000032 -->
### **[[IMM_I000161]]. Residual Learning**

<!-- block:B000033 -->
Let us consider [[IMM_I000007]] as an underlying mapping to be fit by a few stacked layers (not necessarily the entire net), with [[IMM_I000008]] denoting the inputs to the first of these layers. If one hypothesizes that multiple nonlinear layers can asymptotically approximate complicated functions<sup>[[IMM_I000162]]</sup>, then it is equivalent to hypothesize that they can asymptotically approximate the residual functions, _i.e._, [[IMM_I000009]] (assuming that the input and output are of the same dimensions). So rather than expect stacked layers to approximate [[IMM_I000010]], we explicitly let these layers approximate a residual function [[IMM_I000011]]. The original function thus becomes [[IMM_I000012]]. Although both forms should be able to asymptotically approximate the desired functions (as hypothesized), the ease of learning might be different.

<!-- block:B000034 -->
This reformulation is motivated by the counterintuitive phenomena about the degradation problem (Fig. [[IMM_I000163]] left). As we discussed in the introduction, if the added layers can be constructed as identity mappings, a deeper model should have training error no greater than its shallower counterpart. The degradation problem suggests that the solvers might have difficulties in approximating identity mappings by multiple nonlinear layers. With the residual learning reformulation, if identity mappings are optimal, the solvers may simply drive the weights of the multiple nonlinear layers toward zero to approach identity mappings.

<!-- block:B000035 -->
In real cases, it is unlikely that identity mappings are optimal, but our reformulation may help to precondition the problem. If the optimal function is closer to an identity mapping than to a zero mapping, it should be easier for the solver to find the perturbations with reference to an identity mapping, than to learn the function as a new one. We show by experiments (Fig. [[IMM_I000164]]) that the learned residual functions in general have small responses, suggesting that identity mappings provide reasonable preconditioning.
